Sailing to the Stars tested free-flying light sails inside the International Space Station, comparing two deployer designs across six astronaut-run deployments. I helped carry the mechanical design from engineering units to flight hardware and first-authored the mission’s mechanical design paper.

My role

Mechanical subteam, Fall 2024 to Spring 2025. I owned the CAD for the avionics section and its interface to the sail payload, ran engineering-unit tests ahead of the Flight Analysis Readiness Review, led production of the burn-wire filament actuators that release each door, built flight sails, and tied the release line for every flight-unit test.

Why it matters

Light sails are pushed by sunlight alone, and future missions could pair them with gram-scale ChipSats. Little flight data exists on how a sail and its deployer move during release, so the experiment measured both with an onboard IMU and cabin video.

Result

All six sails released on command. Video and IMU data showed the Double-Door deployer was the more stable design, with straighter sail release and no off-axis spike in angular rate. None of the sails opened fully; the team traced this mainly to nitinol temperature and long stowage, which now drives storage tests for the next mission.

Skills applied

  • Tolerance iteration for a rattle-free interface
  • Burn-wire release mechanisms
  • Miura-folded sail assembly with nitinol frames, epoxy bonding
  • Soldering and wire routing
  • Verification testing: pin, separation, shear, vibration
  • Technical writing: first-author conference paper

Technical details
  • Modular architecture. One reusable Avionics Cube mates with swappable Payload Sections, so six deployments needed only three avionics units. I added PCB-holder height so the holder and chassis, not the electrical connector, set the mating fit.
  • Print correction. Prints came out systematically thinner than CAD, so I thickened the PCB holder walls to recover the tight fit.
  • Post-FARR changes. Chamfered screw holes so fasteners sit flush, extended a side panel to cover an exposed latch spring, and raised the avionics shell so a protruding LED no longer kept the unit from sitting level.
  • Sail packing. Each sail is Miura-folded, a rigid-origami pattern that collapses the membrane into a compact stack and lets it open in one motion when the nitinol wires spring back.
  • Filament actuator redesign. Replaced the coiled center wire with a straight diagonal segment on the Double-Door units, so the fishing line always contacts the heated wire and severs reliably.
  • Lesson. Ground tests showed sail orientation had no significant effect on whether a sail deployed. In flight, both unstable runs had the sail folded perpendicular to the door hinge, so fold orientation matters for stability even when release succeeds.

DeSCENT · Cornell SSDSReleasing five ChipSats at rocket apogee

For the ASCENT rocket launch, I designed, assembled and tested a passive two-stage deployer: it ejects from the rocket’s body tube at apogee, then spring force pushes five ChipSats free. I sized the springs to hit a 2–4 m/s exit velocity and verified it on the bench.

My role

Mechanical subteam, Spring 2026. I designed the deployer in Fusion 360 and led its assembly, integration and testing. I am now developing an active-release mechanism that scales the design to 10–20 ChipSats.

Why it matters

ChipSats are gram-scale spacecraft cheap enough to fly in swarms. A suborbital flight is a fast, low-cost way to prove they can be carried through launch and released cleanly, with no power needed for the passive design.

Result

Extensive lab testing with different spring counts confirmed four springs give the target exit velocity without excessive door friction in the body tube. The deployer has not been flight-tested yet.

Skills applied

  • Mechanism design in Fusion 360
  • Spring sizing for exit velocity
  • Design for 3D printing, heat-set inserts
  • Assembly and vehicle integration
  • Deployment testing
Technical details
  • Everything is 3D printed except the four compression springs. The body and ChipSat slots print as one part, split in two halves so the springs can be installed, then epoxied together.
  • Springs are heat-staked into the body and push plate and reinforced with hot glue.
  • Four M4 heat-set inserts in the top of the body take the screws for the coupler and U-bolt assembly.
  • A tether string keeps the door attached to the system after release.

Alpha II · Cornell SSDSLeading a light sail mission from concept review to design

Alpha II will fly a light sail that stays attached to its CubeSat, so the team can image the deployment, track the sail for longer, and finally use it as a drag device to deorbit. As mission lead I wrote the mission statement, objectives, concept of operations and requirements and presented them at the Mission Concept Review in May 2026.

My role

Mission Lead and Mechanical Lead for a nine-person student team. I also derive the mechanical requirements and research concepts for tethering the sail after deployment.

Why it matters

Alpha flew in 2026 and proved a free-flying sail could deploy from a 1U CubeSat, but very few packets came back, all within about eight hours, and almost nothing about the deployment itself. Keeping the sail attached turns a one-shot release into a longer, observable experiment.

Mission objectives

Primary. Verify sail and ChipSat deployment while attached in low Earth orbit; image the deployment and downlink it; receive ChipSat data on the ground.

Extended. Use the ChipSats as the sail’s avionics package; demonstrate laser communications by retroreflection off the sail; deorbit with the sail as a drag device.

Skills applied

  • Mission design and CONOPS
  • Objectives and requirements flowdown
  • Design reviews (MCR, SRR, PDR)
  • Leading a cross-subsystem team
  • Reusing flight-proven (TRL 9) hardware
  1. MCRMay 2026
  2. SRRSep 2026
  3. TODAY ▾PDRNov–Dec 2026

Technical details
  • Avionics. Replace Alpha’s analog current sensor (INA169), which read 250 mA of solar current on the dark side of orbit, with a digital INA219 that tested within about 10%. Switch to new solar cells because Alpha’s are no longer made.
  • Mechanical. Rework the sail compartment and the sail itself so the sail stays tethered after deployment.
  • Attitude control. Bring back Alpha’s magnetorquer pointing algorithm and add modes for laser pointing and drag. The team compared single-magnetorquer torque (≈3.3 × 10⁻⁶ N·m) with sail drag torque at 400 km (≈4.1 × 10⁻⁶ N·m) to size the magnetorquers.
  • Top risks. Sail separation and attitude control failure (medium), sail failing to unfurl (low).

To test light sail deployment in near-space, I did the mechanical and electrical integration of the Alpha CubeSat for a high-altitude balloon flight and ran the thermal tests that showed a cold sail would not fully unfurl.

My role

Mechanical subteam, Fall 2025, working on an existing deployer design. I installed new solar panels, modified and reprinted a door latch that failed during integration, mounted the cameras and tether on the suspension frame, debugged the wiring harness against the Altium schematics, verified every sensor, and ran and analyzed the thermal tests.

Result

Deployment tests succeeded once the burn wire loops were kept clear of the coils. Thermal testing showed the 3D-printed chassis got cold enough to stop the sail unfurling, so we added foam and then masking tape lining to insulate the sail and heater compartments. The balloon flight has not happened yet.

Skills applied

  • Mechanical and electrical integration
  • Part modification and reprinting
  • Fastener and spacer selection
  • Electrical debugging with schematics
  • Arduino sensor verification
  • Thermal testing and analysis

Drivetrain & Vibration Feeder · Cornell NexusSecuring a loose drivetrain and driving a feeder from both sides

On the Cornell Nexus project team, I fixed front wheels that kept working loose by moving their weight off the shaft couplers onto a secure plate, and reworked the vibration feeder so it is driven from both sides and mounts directly to the filtration subsystem.

My role

Mechanical subteam, Sep 2025 to May 2026, on a student team of more than 30. Nexus builds Honu, an autonomous, solar-powered robot that drives along beaches and filters out microplastics.

The problem

The front wheels carried their weight on the shaft couplers, which loosened in use. The vibration feeder was driven from one side only.

Result

The front wheels are reattached and secured to a plate. The feeder now runs from a motor on each end, with a new clamping eccentric disk, and replaces an existing T-slot member on the filtration frame. The new feeder has not been tested on the full system yet.

Skills applied

  • Root-cause analysis of a loosening joint
  • Load path redesign
  • Crank-slider mechanism design
  • Designing into an existing frame
  • SolidWorks CAD
Technical details
  • Vibration comes from a crank-slider mechanism: an eccentric disk on each motor shaft drives a crank arm that shakes the feeder tray.
  • The intermediate design tried to drive the unactuated side through a long axle with a second crank arm and eccentric disk. The team ruled it out: a long axle would not be stiff enough to transmit the torque needed, and it added friction and misalignment problems.
  • The final feeder bolts directly to the filtration frame in place of an existing T-slot member.
  • Next step: tighter integration with the filtration subsystem.

  1. 2026 Sailing to the Stars ISS Experiment: Results and Conclusions from Light Sail Deployments in Microgravity40th Annual Small Satellite Conference · Logan, UT · SSC26-P2-42Co-authorAbstractSailing to the Stars is an experiment led by students at Cornell University to deploy free-flying light sails within the microgravity environment of the International Space Station. As this light sail architecture is relatively new, additional insights on the sail deployment dynamics and deployer design are needed to guide future use of this technology. The hardware launched to the International Space Station with Crew-11 on August 1st, 2025. Astronauts followed the developed procedures to conduct six light sail deployments in total from two different deployer designs. During each test, data was recorded from the Inertial Measurement Unit within each deployer along with video footage of the deployments. This paper assesses the data and shares useful insights from the experiment execution. An analysis of each of the six deployments determines which deployer concept is most stable and recommended for future missions.Conference posterClick to enlargePoster PDF ↗ Paper PDF ↗
  2. 2025 Unfurling Light Sails for Advanced Deployment Technology: Sailing to the Stars Mission Mechanical Design7th International Symposium on Space Sailing · TU Delft, The NetherlandsFirst authorAbstractSailing to the Stars is a student-led mission at Cornell University designed to investigate the deployment of free-flying light sails in microgravity using the International Space Station (ISS) as a testing platform. Crew aboard the ISS will carry out six deployments using two distinct deployer designs. This paper outlines the mechanical design of the 3D-printed light sail deployers used for the experiment. These Payload Sections are available in single-door and double-door variants, both of which interface with the Avionics Cube. A modular approach enabled the same avionics housing to be mated with all six sail enclosures, reducing manufacture time and system upmass. Details of light sail assembly and sail deployment mechanisms are also provided. Finally, an overview of subsystem and system-level testing demonstrates readiness for launch on SpaceX Crew-11 in summer 2025. The team shall analyze downlinked video footage and downmassed IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit) data to assess the post-deployment stability of both the sail and the deployer, comparing the performance of each design.
